PathWars strategies

Quoridor looks like a race and plays like an auction: every wall is a bid, and the winner is whoever spends wisely. These are the ideas that actually decide games — learned from watching thousands of real matches.

▶ Try them now

1. Count both paths, every turn

The single most important habit: before every move, count your shortest road to the goal and the rival's. A wall is good only if it lengthens their road more than the move it costs you. A wall that adds +3 to their path while your own path stays the same is a great deal; a wall that adds +1 while giving them a new idea is a waste. The game rewards this directly — short decisive games and clean wins climb fastest, while burnt walls leave you with nothing to run with.

2. Walls are money — don't spend early

You start rich (10 walls) and finish poor (0). Early walls are usually the worst ones: the board is empty, the rival's plan is unknown, and almost any wall can simply be walked around. The strongest pattern from real games:

  • Moves 1–4: run. Take the centre or mirror the rival; spend at most one wall, and only to answer a direct threat.
  • Midgame: spend when roads cross — bend the rival sideways where they are already committed.
  • Endgame (3 rows to go): stop walling unless a single wall wins on the spot. Every wall now is a step you didn't take.

3. Play the tempo, not the chase

If your road is shorter than the rival's, run — do not spend moves slowing someone who is already behind. Each wall you place is a move the rival gets for free. The classic blunder: a player two steps ahead spends three moves building a beautiful maze, and loses by one step. Chase only when you are behind or level — that is when every extra step on their road is worth your move.

You need 4 steps, rival needs 6. Walling (+2 for them) vs running: Wall: you 4, them 8 → you win by 4, but spent 1 wall + 1 move. Run: you 3, them 6 → you win by 3, wall saved for later. RUN.

4. Own the centre early

The middle columns are the crossroads of every Duel board. A pawn in the centre has options; a pawn squeezed to the edge has one road that a single wall can bend. Don't drift sideways without a reason — and if the rival drifts, that is exactly where your first wall should point.

5. Endgame math wins games

When both pawns are close to the goal, stop thinking in walls and start thinking in numbers. Count precisely: my steps vs their steps, whose move it is. Remember the jump — standing next to the rival near the goal is often a free two-square leap that flips the count. If the numbers say you win by running, run, even if a tempting wall is glowing.

6. Upset hunting (for rating)

A win pays 25 points, but beating someone ranked above you pays 40 — while losing to a weaker rival costs 20 instead of 10. If you want to climb: play opponents at or above your level, and don't farm rematches — from the fourth straight game against the same person points halve, and from the seventh they stop entirely. Change opponent and full scoring returns.

7. Four-player tables play differently

  • Don't lead early. The leader gets walled by three people. Stay second or third until the final stretch.
  • Walls are scarce (7). Spend them on the actual leader, not on grudges.
  • Watch eliminations. A player who misses moves is out and their walls stay — dead walls reshape everyone's road for free.
  • The centre is one cell. Races are decided by who steps in first, so count turn order, not just distance: being closest means nothing if two rivals move before you.

8. Train with the right AI

Easy forgives, Normal punishes hanging mistakes, Hard exploits weak walls, and Hardcore simply out-counts you. Practice a new idea against Easy, test it against Hard, and measure yourself against Hardcore — details in the AI guide. Practice games never touch your rating, so experiment freely.
